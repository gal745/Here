    Module 1

    Why this matters: body, mind and spirit

    ~8 minSelf-check

    Most of us now spend 7 to 10 waking hours a day looking at a screen. AI tools add a new kind of load on top of that: they are fast, always available, and they invite us to hand over not just chores but our thinking. That can be a gift or a slow loss, depending on how we use them.

    This course looks at three layers of health, because each one is affected differently:

    Body

    Eyes, posture, movement

    Close focus for hours, fewer blinks, evening light that delays sleep, and long periods of stillness.

    Mind

    Attention, memory, judgment

    Mental fatigue from constant evaluation, and skills that fade when we stop using them.

    Spirit

    Meaning, presence, connection

    Intention, quiet, creativity and relationships that need unhurried, human time.

    The core idea of this course

    AI is your employee, not your manager. You set direction, make the decisions, and keep the creative and moral work. AI does well-defined tasks, and you check its work. Every habit in this course supports that relationship.

    Module 2

    Eyes and light: blue light, eye breaks and looking far

    ~12 minPractice: 20-20-20Quiz

    What actually tires your eyes

    "Digital eye strain" (also called computer vision syndrome) shows up as tired, dry, burning or blurry eyes, headaches and a stiff neck. Three things cause most of it:

    1. Holding close focus. To see a screen at 50 to 70 cm, a small ring muscle inside the eye (the ciliary muscle) stays contracted. Hours of that is like holding a light weight at arm's length all day.
    2. Blinking less. People blink roughly half as often while concentrating on a screen, and many blinks are incomplete. The tear film dries out.
    3. A poor setup. Glare, a screen brighter or darker than the room, text that is too small, and a monitor set too high all add strain.
    Blue light: what the evidence says

    Screens are not damaging your retina. A phone or monitor gives off a small fraction of the blue light you get from being outdoors on a cloudy day. A 2023 Cochrane review found that blue-light-filtering glasses probably do not reduce eye strain from computer use.

    Where blue light does matter is sleep. Bright, blue-rich light in the evening tells your brain it is still daytime. It suppresses melatonin and can push back your sleep. That is a real effect on your body, and it builds up night after night.

    Evening light habits

    Turn on Night Shift, Night Light or a warm display filter from sunset. Dim screens and room lights after dark. Aim for 60 minutes screen-free before bed, and keep the phone charging outside the bedroom.

    Morning light habits

    Get 10 to 20 minutes of outdoor daylight soon after waking, even when it is cloudy. Morning light sets your body clock, so you feel sleepy at the right time later. For children, time outdoors is also linked to lower rates of short-sightedness.

    The 20-20-20 rule, and why green and far away help

    Every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 feet (6 metres) away for at least 20 seconds. Distance lets the focusing muscle relax fully. Blink slowly a few times while you do it to re-wet your eyes.

    If you can, look out of a window at trees, a garden, the sky or a far horizon. Green, natural scenes do more than rest the eyes. In a 2015 study from the University of Melbourne, people who glanced at a green roof for 40 seconds made fewer errors on a following attention task than people who looked at a concrete roof. Researchers link this to "attention restoration": nature holds our attention softly, so the effortful kind can recover.

    Twenty minutes can feel frequent. Treat the 20-20-20 break as a tiny habit that costs 20 seconds. Then, every 1 to 2 hours, take a longer "green break" of 5 to 10 minutes, ideally outside or at a window, with no screen at all.

    Practice · 20 seconds

    Do one now

    Find the farthest point you can see, preferably something green. Start the timer and keep your eyes there until it ends.

    Set up your screen

    • Distance: about an arm's length (50 to 70 cm) from your eyes.
    • Height: top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, so you look slightly down.
    • Brightness: match the brightness of the room. A glowing screen in a dark room is hard on the eyes.
    • Glare: put the screen side-on to windows, not facing them or with them behind it.
    • Text size: make it larger than you think you need. Squinting is a sign to zoom in.
    • Dry eyes: blink on purpose, keep air vents away from your face, and ask a pharmacist about preservative-free lubricating drops.
    • Checkups: an uncorrected prescription causes strain that no habit will fix. Have an eye exam every one to two years.

    Module 3

    Body: posture and micro-movement breaks

    ~10 minPractice: 2-minute routineQuiz

    The problem with long screen sessions is less about "bad posture" and more about staying still for too long. Muscles and joints like variety. Any position gets uncomfortable if you hold it for hours. The best posture is your next one.

    A micro-movement break takes one to three minutes. You do it every 30 to 60 minutes, and it needs no equipment or change of clothes. Short, frequent breaks help circulation, blood sugar and stiffness more than one long workout at the end of a day spent sitting.

    Practice · 2 minutes

    Follow a guided micro-movement break

    The coach walks you through six movements, 20 seconds each. Move gently and stay within a pain-free range.

    Build movement into the work itself
    • Stand up while the AI is generating a long answer.
    • Take phone calls and voice notes standing or walking.
    • Keep your water glass small, so refilling it becomes a regular walk.
    • Alternate sitting and standing if you have an adjustable desk. Standing all day is not the goal either.

    Module 4

    Mind: why extended AI use is exhausting, and how to rest

    ~12 minBreak coach setupQuiz

    Many people notice that a long session with an AI assistant leaves them more drained than the same hours of ordinary work. It can feel like you did very little and yet you are exhausted. There are good reasons for this.

    Constant evaluation

    Every answer has to be read, judged and checked. You become a full-time reviewer, and reviewing is effortful work.

    No natural pauses

    Answers arrive in seconds, so the small rests that slower work gives you (waiting, walking, looking things up) disappear.

    Too many options

    AI can generate ten versions of anything. Choosing between endless options causes decision fatigue.

    Context switching

    Jumping between chats, tabs and tasks has a real cost. Each switch leaves part of your attention behind.

    Signs you need a break now
    • You are re-reading the same paragraph or answer.
    • You accept outputs without really checking them.
    • You are stuck in a "just one more prompt" loop.
    • You feel irritable, restless, or your jaw and shoulders are tight.
    • You cannot remember what you did in the last hour.

    A rhythm that works

    1. Start with your own head. Spend 5 minutes with pen and paper on the goal and your first ideas before opening any AI tool.
    2. Work in time boxes of 25 to 50 minutes. Decide what you want out of each block before it starts.
    3. Take a 20-second eye break every 20 minutes and a 2 to 5 minute movement break at the end of each block.
    4. Every 90 minutes or so, step away for 10 to 20 minutes. Go outside, eat, talk to someone, or sit quietly. Scrolling a phone is not a break for the mind or the eyes.
    5. End each session with a recap. Write three lines: what you did, what you decided, and what is next. Then ask the AI to quiz you on it (module 6).
    6. Set a daily limit. Decide when intensive AI work ends for the day, and protect some screen-free hours.

    Module 5

    Keep your thinking: AI as employee, not manager

    ~15 minExercise: delegation sorterQuiz

    Skills you stop using get weaker. Researchers call handing mental work to a tool cognitive offloading. It is often useful: we offload phone numbers to our contacts list and arithmetic to calculators. The risk is offloading the capacities that make us who we are.

    Early research points the same way. A 2025 survey of knowledge workers by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon found that the more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they reported doing. A 2025 MIT Media Lab study found that people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed lower brain engagement and remembered less of what "they" had written. These studies are small and early, but the direction is consistent with what we know about learning: effort is how the brain builds skill.

    Three things to keep for yourself

    Creativity. Generate your own ideas first. Your first rough idea is where your voice lives. Use AI later to stretch, test or polish, not to replace the spark.

    Decision-making. AI can lay out options and trade-offs. The choice, and responsibility for it, stays with you. Decisions are a skill, and you keep it only by making them.

    Discernment. Judging what is true, good, wise and right for your life cannot be delegated. AI can be confidently wrong, and it does not know your values, your body or your relationships.

    You are the manager
    • Set the goal and why it matters
    • Generate the first ideas
    • Decide between options
    • Judge quality, truth and fit
    • Own the result and its consequences
    AI is the employee
    • Drafts, formats and summarizes
    • Lists options and trade-offs you asked for
    • Does repetitive or mechanical steps
    • Argues against your idea when asked
    • Reports back for review, never ships on its own

    How a good manager briefs an employee

    • Give a clear task, not a vague wish. "Summarize these notes into five bullet points for my team" rather than "help me with this."
    • State what you have already decided. This keeps the AI from quietly deciding for you.
    • Ask for options, not answers, when a judgment call is involved.
    • Review everything. You would not sign a document an intern wrote without reading it.
    • Notice when you are asking to be told what to do. Questions like "what should I do?" or "which is better?" are your cue to decide for yourself.

    Module 6

    Stay sharp: ask AI to quiz you

    ~10 min6 copy-ready promptsQuiz

    The same tool that can make you mentally lazy can make you sharper. The difference is whether it does your thinking or makes you think.

    One of the most reliable findings in learning science is the testing effect: pulling information out of your memory strengthens it far more than re-reading it. In a well-known 2006 study by Roediger and Karpicke, students who practised recalling a text remembered much more a week later than students who re-read it several times.

    So after you learn or create something with AI, turn the tables: have it question you. Answer before you look anything up. Being a little wrong is part of how it works.

    When to use these
    • At the end of any AI session longer than 30 minutes
    • After AI helps you with code, a document, or research you will need to defend
    • The next morning, to check what stuck
    • Before you rely on a new skill without the tool

    Module 7

    Spirit: intention, presence and unplugged time

    ~10 minReflectionQuiz

    Whatever "spirit" means to you (faith, meaning, inner quiet, connection to nature or to other people), it needs things that screens tend to crowd out: silence, slowness, presence and real contact. You don't have to give technology up. You need to decide where it stops.

    Open with intention

    Before you open an app or a chat, pause for one breath and name why you are opening it. If you can't name a reason, you may not need to open it.

    Protect the edges of the day

    Keep the first 30 to 60 minutes after waking and the last hour before sleep screen-free. Use them for light, movement, prayer, reflection, journaling or people.

    Keep relationships human

    Don't let AI write your words of love, grief, apology or gratitude. They matter because they cost you something and come from you.

    Make things by hand

    Write, draw, cook, garden or play music without a screen. Creating with your hands grounds you in your body and in the present.

    Keep a weekly unplugged block

    Choose a few hours or a whole day each week with no AI and minimal screens. Many traditions keep a sabbath. The idea works for anyone.

    Let yourself be bored

    Boredom and quiet are where insight, rest and self-knowledge show up. Try not to fill every pause with input.

    Reflection · 3 minutes, off screen if you can

    Three questions to answer honestly

    1. When did technology last make my life genuinely better? When did it last leave me feeling emptier?
    2. What do I want to always do myself, even if AI could do it faster?
    3. What would I do with one screen-free hour a day?
